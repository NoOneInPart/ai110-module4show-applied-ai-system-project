# 🎧 Model Card: musicmachine 0.001a-rc2

## 1. Model Name  

> musicmachine 0.001a-rc2

---

## 2. Intended Use  

> This recommender generates song recommendations based on a user prompt that is fed to Gemini Flash Lite to produce a user taste profile. It assumes that the user prefers songs of similar energy over a hard preference for a particular genre or mood, and there is some weight for songs that match a desired valence, danceability, and tempo. It is more for exploration than for production use.

---

## 3. How the Model Works 

> The genre, mood, energy, and acoustic metadata are used when recommending songs. Up to 5 score points are given to a song based on how well it matches a user's preferred energy level, 1 score point is given if the song matches a user's preferred mood and genre respectively, and up to 0.5 points are awarded depending on how acoustic a song is and whether a user likes acoustic songs. Originally, the acoustic score was a fixed bonus depending on whether a song passed a threshold of 0.5 acousticness, but now it scales depending on how acoustic the song is. 

> **Update:** The profile and scorer now also support three optional continuous preferences — tempo, valence (how positive/happy a song sounds), and danceability. Each works like the energy term, rewarding songs whose value is close to the user's target, but carries a lighter weight (1.5 each vs. energy's 5), so energy is still the backbone. Any preference the user leaves blank is simply ignored, so a partial profile still produces recommendations.

---

## 4. Data  

> There are 20 songs in the catalog, it covers pop/lofi/rock/ambient/jazz/synthwave/alternative/metal/psychedelic/disco/folk/hip-hop/classical/house/country/r&b genres and happy/chill/intense/moody/relaxed/focused/confident/calm/energetic/melancholic/romatic moods. 10 songs were added and partially fictionally generated by Claude Opus. Mid-energy songs and any other genre/mood that does not appear above are missing from the dataset.

> **Update:** The catalog was rebuilt and is now **88 real songs** (in `data/song2.csv`), sourced from the public-domain (CC0) **Spotify Tracks Dataset – Audio Features** on Kaggle: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/saichaitanyareddyai/spotify-tracks-dataset-audio-features . The audio-feature columns (energy, tempo, valence, danceability, acousticness) are **real measured values** from that dataset rather than estimates, spread across a wide range of genres with an even energy distribution, so the old mid-energy gap is gone. Two caveats are kept on purpose as a demonstration of how imperfect data flows into recommendations: (1) `mood` is **not** in the source data — it is derived from each song's valence and energy, so it sometimes disagrees with human perception (Joy Division's *Disorder* comes out "happy"); and (2) the dataset's genre tags are noisy — each track is labelled by the single playlist it was scraped from — so some genres are wrong (the Eagles land under "folk").

---

## 5. Strengths  

> This model works best for users who have strong preferences for continuous audio qualities like energy, tempo, valence (mood positivity), or danceability. Because target energy carries the heaviest scoring weight (5.0), energy similarity acts as the primary backbone, ensuring recommended songs closely match the desired intensity level. At the same time, optional factors (valence, danceability, tempo at 1.5 each; genre and mood at 1.0 each) allow the system to fine-tune results—for example, ranking high-energy pop or energetic/upbeat tracks at the top without strictly disqualifying songs that miss an exact genre label.
> 
> It is also really good at coming up with user taste profiles from very abstract prompts, like "music for snakes," thanks to the use of Gemini as an external LLM.

---

## 6. Limitations and Bias 

> Genres and moods use exact-string matching so nearby genres and moods, like pop -> indie pop, are still considered completely separate. The new song catalog skews Western, popular, and recent (recognizable songs were preferred), the source dataset's genre labels are unreliable, and valence-derived moods can be counterintuitive.

## 8. Future Work  

> The most obvious new feature I can think of is to make the tool agentic. Basically, allow Gemini to ask for more info, banter with the user about their prompt, and allow the user to refine their prompt. This does open up the program to a greater chance of AI misuse, so a guardrail mechanism has to be implemented to stop the program if the chat goes off the rails. Also, probably a paid API plan to increase rate limits.

## 9. Personal Reflection  

> I learned that recommendation systems are very fickle and flatten everything about a song to metrics like energy and "danceability," which eliminates a lot of nuance about what makes a song good, yet it is interesting that it still kinda works. The rule-based retrieval system is very cheap to run and transparent, but is too simple; it takes genre and mood names literally and any variation is treated as something completely different, and it relies too much on retrieving songs of a similar energy level, so Gemini has a limited variety of songs it can choose from to recommend. I can see the appeal of training end-to-end machine learning models to do recommendations, though the algorithm really does turn into a black box at that point. I have a bit more respect for music recommendation algorithms in apps like Spotify now, it's just trying to do its best with what it has.🥲

#### Limitations & Biases
> As far as I know I managed to get a catalog of 88 songs that equally span the energy spectrum so I'm unaware of bias, but there is a limitation in data quality. For example, Joy Division's "Disorder" is identified as having a "happy" mood, which is debatable, and if Gemini doesn't catch the questionable choice during the RAG part, it could get recommended in the wrong context if the song otherwise scores high enough or it might not be recommended in the right context.

#### Misuse & Prevention
> The AI could theoretically be misused, but the user is only allowed to enter one prompt per program execution so it can't become a free chat, and the system prompt encourages Gemini to refuse to answer if it isn't relevant to the program. In order for it to be misused, an initial user prompt must be formatted as a one-shot that manages to "jailbreak" and bypass the system prompt and then return the desired information. There's a bit of a hurdle right there.

#### Reliability Surprises
> I was surprised that Gemini could manage to respond to and build a profile for the most abstract of prompts, like "give me music for snakes," to which it responds with *"Got it, let's go with some slippery, smooth, and mysterious vibes."* It was pretty good at not helping with anything else, like in the README where I asked it "how do i get my horse back from my ex-wife," to which it responded that it can't help with legal advice.
